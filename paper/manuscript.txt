Hankel-Norm and Information Limits on Delay Detection by Low-Order State-Space Models, with Application to Graph Random Walks

Olivia Kim and Aryan Padarthi

Abstract

An order-S linear time-invariant (LTI) state-space model must decide whether
the current sample repeats the one k steps earlier, as random-walk graph
learners do to detect cycles. For Gaussian inputs, exact detection requires
S ≥ k + 1 and a singular state matrix, so invertible dynamics such as S4D and
Mamba cannot achieve it. Since the lag-k delay has k unit Hankel singular
values, for S < k no linear probe beats the zero probe in Hankel norm, any
probe explains at most S/k of the delayed sample’s variance, and every readout
has detection divergence at most $1/2\ln\frac{k}{k-S}$. Optimal fits track the
S/k law, and a trained shift register switches on at S = k + 1. Trained
diagonal SSMs need 4.0× the minimal state; frozen optimal poles remove most of
this overhead for k ≤ 12. On circular skip-link graphs the remaining gap lies
in the readout.

Index Terms: State-space models, Hankel operators, model order reduction,
detection, random walks on graphs.

Introduction

Deciding whether a signal repeats itself after a lag k is a basic detection
task: it is echo detection, and it is the primitive that sequence models must
implement to recall earlier inputs. Structured state-space models (SSMs) [1,
2, 3] implement it with a recursive filter of order S, and much of their
design is about approximating delays with few states [4, 5]. That the pure
delay z^(−k) has McMillan degree k is classical realisation theory [6, 7, 8].
What is missing is a rate: how much of the delayed sample can an order-S state
carry when S < k, and what does that imply for detection by an arbitrary
nonlinear readout? Related limits for recurrent models concern memory decay
and optimisation [9], copying and associative recall [10, 11], and state
tracking [12].

Our application is graph learning. Message-passing GNNs are bounded by the
1-WL test [13, 14] and cannot separate the regular circular skip-link (CSL)
graphs [15, 16]. Walk-based learners read random walks as sequences [17, 18,
19], and random node features [20, 21] turn walk revisits, and hence closed
walks, into token repetitions. SSMs are now used as the sequence backbone of
such models [22, 23, 18].

Our contributions are: (i) exact limits: for Gaussian inputs, exact lag-k
detection needs S ≥ k + 1 and a singular state matrix, and is impossible for
invertible dynamics at any S; (ii) rates: for S < k, no linear probe beats the
zero probe in Hankel norm, the explained variance is at most S/k, and for
Gaussian inputs this bounds the divergence available to every readout; (iii)
graph consequence: exact non-backtracking (NB) return horizons W^(*)(r) at
which window features separate each CSL class; and (iv) validation: optimal
fits, shift-register and frozen-pole controls, and seeded experiments that
separate the information limit, the optimisation of the dynamics and the
readout.

Problem Setting

A sequence model with real state s_(t) ∈ ℝ^(S) reads tokens x_(t) ∈ ℝ^(d):
s_(t) = A_(t)s_(t − 1) + B_(t)x_(t),   ŷ_(t) = g(s_(t),x_(t)),
with an arbitrary readout g. It is LTI if A_(t) ≡ A and B_(t) ≡ B. The lag-k
repeat label is y_(t) = 1[x_(t)=x_(t − k)]. In the graph setting, v₀, v₁, … is
an NB walk (v_(t + 1) ≠ v_(t − 1)), each walk draws fresh features
f_(v) ∼ 𝒩(0,I_(d)) i.i.d. over nodes, and x_(t) = f_(v_(t)). Then
y_(t) = 1[v_(t)=v_(t − k)] almost surely, i.e. y_(t) = 1 exactly when the last
k steps form a closed NB walk. Their frequencies follow from powers of the
Hashimoto (NB) operator [24, 25].

Limits

Let x_(t) be i.i.d. 𝒩(0,I_(d)) with a stationary past, and test H₁:
x_(t) = x_(t − k) against H₀: x_(t) fresh, from (s_(t),x_(t)) of an LTI model.
(a) If S ≤ k, or (b) if A is invertible (any S), every readout g has Bayes
error  > 0. (c) If S ≥ k + 1, a singular A suffices: the shift register
s_(t) = (w^(⊤)x_(t),…,w^(⊤)x_(t − S + 1)) with
g = 1[w^(⊤)x_(t)=(s_(t))_(k + 1)] has zero error for every w ≠ 0.

Proof. Under both hypotheses (s_(t),x_(t)) is zero-mean Gaussian, so the two
laws are either equivalent (error  > 0) or mutually singular. Singularity
needs a^(⊤)s_(t) + b^(⊤)x_(t) = 0 a.s. under H₁ but not under H₀. Since
s_(t − 1) has the same law under both, this means
a^(⊤)As_(t − 1) = v^(⊤)x_(t − k) a.s. with v =  − (B^(⊤)a+b) ≠ 0,
i.e. a^(⊤)A^(m)B = δ_(m, k)v^(⊤) for all m ≥ 1: an exact FIR realisation of
v^(⊤)z^(−k) by (A,AB,a). (a) Its Hankel matrix has rank k, so S ≥ k. At S = k
the realisation is minimal, hence nilpotent and controllable through AB; but
[AB,…,A^(k)B] = A[B,…,A^(k − 1)B] has rank below k for nilpotent A. (b) If A
is invertible, a^(⊤)A^(k + 1)A^(j)B = 0 for all j ≥ 0 makes a orthogonal to
A^(k + 1)ℛ = ℛ, the reachable space, so v = 0. (c) Under H₀,
w^(⊤)x_(t) ≠ w^(⊤)x_(t − k) a.s.

Exactness over all token sequences is stronger: it needs S ≥ (k+1)d, because
the subspaces A^(j)Bℝ^(d), j = 0, …, k, must be independent. Every S4D or
Mamba discretisation A = exp (ΔΛ) is invertible, so exact detection is never
available to them. The relevant question is how well an order-S state can
carry the lag-k sample. A linear probe z_(t) = c^(⊤)s_(t) + β^(⊤)x_(t)
estimates w^(⊤)x_(t − k) with ∥w∥ = 1. Its error
z_(t) − w^(⊤)x_(t − k) = ∑_(m ≥ 0)e_(m)x_(t − m) has taps
e_(m) = c^(⊤)A^(m)B − δ_(m, k)w^(⊤) for m ≥ 1 and transfer function
E(z) = ∑_(m ≥ 1)e_(m)z^(−m), with ∥E(e^(jω))∥ the Euclidean norm of the 1 × d
row. Let ℋ_(k)(f) be the k × kd block-Hankel section with (i,j) block
f_(i + j + 1), 0 ≤ i, j < k.

If S < k, every LTI probe satisfies
$$\begin{aligned}
 &\|\mathcal{H}_k(e)\|_2\ \ge\ 1,\quad\text{hence}\quad \textstyle\sup_\omega\|E(e^{j\omega})\|\ \ge\ 1, \label{eq:hinf}\\
 &\|\mathcal{H}_k(e)\|_F^2=\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{2k-1}\min(m,2k-m)\|e_m\|^2\ \ge\ k-S. \label{eq:frob}
\end{aligned}$$
Hence, for i.i.d. tokens with 𝔼[xx^(⊤)] = I_(d) and a stationary past (or
t ≥ 2k − 1), 𝔼[(z_(t)−w^(⊤)x_(t − k))²] ≥ 1 − S/k: the probe explains at most
a fraction S/k of the variance. For S = k the infimum of the error is 0, but
it is not attained.

Proof. Since h_(i + j + 1) = (c^(⊤)A^(i))(A^(j + 1)B), we have
rank ℋ_(k)(h) ≤ S. The target δ_(m, k)w^(⊤) gives a block anti-diagonal matrix
with k unit singular values. The Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem [26, 27] in the
spectral and Frobenius norms gives ∥ℋ_(k)(e)∥₂ ≥ σ_(S + 1) = 1 and
∥ℋ_(k)(e)∥_(F)² ≥ ∑_(i > S)σ_(i)² = k − S. A finite section is bounded by the
Hankel operator Γ_(E), and ∥Γ_(E)∥ ≤ ∥E∥_(∞) because Γ_(E) is a compression of
the Laurent operator of E. This gives 2; Nehari’s theorem [28] sharpens it to
∥Γ_(E)∥ = dist(E,H⁻^(∞)). The tap e_(m) occurs min (m,2k−m) ≤ k times, which
gives 3 and the variance bound. For S = k, take A = γP with P the k-cycle
permutation, B = u₁w^(⊤), c = γ^(−k)u₁ and β =  − γ^(−k)w. Then
e_(m) = γ^(m − k)w^(⊤) for m ∈ {2k, 3k, …} and 0 otherwise, so the error is
γ^(2k)/(1−γ^(2k)) → 0 as γ → 0. Zero error would be an exact FIR realisation,
which the proof of Proposition 1(a) excludes at S = k.

Bound 2 says that for S < k no LTI probe beats outputting zero in Hankel norm:
all Hankel singular values of z^(−k) equal one, so its optimal Hankel-norm
approximant of degree  < k is zero [8, 7]. Bound 3 is the rate. Under Gaussian
inputs it extends to every readout.

Let x_(t) be i.i.d. 𝒩(0,I_(d)) and S < k. For every measurable g, the minimum
mean-squared error of x_(t − k) given (s_(t),x_(t)) is at least d − S/k. For
H₁: x_(t) = x_(t − k) against H₀: x_(t) fresh, the observations satisfy
$\mathrm{KL}(P_1\|P_0)\le1/2\ln\frac{k}{k-S}$, so no detector exceeds balanced
accuracy $1/2+1/2\big(1/4\ln\frac{k}{k-S}\big)^{1/2}$.

Proof. For jointly Gaussian variables the conditional mean is linear. Stacking
d probes gives a target with kd unit Hankel singular values, and 3 yields
total error  ≥ d − S/k. Next, (s_(t),x_(t)) is a function of
(s_(t − 1),x_(t)). These are independent under H₀ and have the same marginals
under H₁, so KL = I(s_(t − 1);x_(t − k)) =  − 1/2∑_(i)ln (1−λ_(i)), where
λ_(i) are the squared canonical correlations. Relative to s_(t − 1), x_(t − k)
is the lag-(k−1) tap, with the m = 0 tap included. The k × k section with
blocks c^(⊤)A^(i + j)B, 0 ≤ i, j < k, again has a target with k unit singular
values, and tap m occurs min (m+1,2k−1−m) ≤ k times. Hence ∑_(i)λ_(i) ≤ S/k.
By convexity the maximum puts all mass on one λ_(i). Data processing and
Pinsker’s inequality complete the proof.

The bounds limit precision, not information. For S < k the state can still
correlate with the lagged sample, so statistics pooled over many tokens remain
informative. The near-optimal realisation at S = k needs gains γ^(−k) and is
exponentially ill-conditioned (cf. [9]). Input-dependent (A_(t),B_(t)) break
the factorisation used above, so we test them empirically. On walks, revisits
make tokens dependent. Eq. 2 is distribution-free; the variance and divergence
rates use the i.i.d. surrogate, which we check in Section IV.

NB return horizons. Let p_(G)(ℓ) = Pr [v_(ℓ)=v₀] for the stationary NB walk
(uniform on directed edges). We compute it exactly from the Hashimoto operator
[24]. For a family {G_(r)}, let W^(*)(r) be the smallest W such that
max_(ℓ ≤ W)|p_(G_(r))(ℓ)−p_(G_(r′))(ℓ)| > τ for all r′ ≠ r. It is the shortest
window of revisit frequencies that separates class r. For the ten CSL classes
(n = 41, skips r ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 11, 12, 13, 16}), W^(*)(r) ranges from 3
to 8 and is unchanged for τ ∈ [10⁻⁴,3⋅10⁻³] (Table I). W^(*) is a horizon for
exact access. An order-S reader with S < W^(*) still explains up to S/ℓ of the
lag-ℓ variance. We therefore expect pooled estimators to succeed below W^(*),
at a sample cost that grows as S/W^(*) shrinks, since each token carries
divergence of order S/(2ℓ).

Experiments

Models. Our learned SSM is one diagonal complex layer with S4D-Lin
initialisation [2]. It has ⌊S/2⌋ complex modes plus one real mode if S is odd,
so its real state dimension is exactly S. The dynamics are A = exp (ΔΛ) and
B_(t) = ΔB, with Δ learned per mode (LTI) or set to
Δ_(t) = softplus(W_(Δ)x_(t)+v_(Δ)) (input-dependent step, the Mamba-style
gate). The readout sees [ℜs_(t),ℑs_(t),x_(t)] and comparison features
(Ux_(t)−Vs_(t))^(⊙2), followed by a two-layer MLP. We use d = 8 features,
AdamW with a cosine schedule, and 3 seeds; intervals are 95% t-intervals. All
runs are on CPU, and a script regenerates every number and figure in this
letter.

Delay realisation. We fit an S-state diagonal impulse response to δ_(m, k)
(m ≥ 1), keeping the best of four restarts (Fig. 1). All 16 fits with S < k
respect $\|e\|_2\ge\sqrt{1-S/k}$, with a median excess MSE of 0.11 over the
bound. The error first drops to about 0.1 at a median of S = 1.0 k, so the
rate of Theorem 1 is nearly attained by direct fitting.

Token-level detection. We train on NB walks (T = 64) over 256 random 4-regular
graphs (n = 24, unions of two Hamiltonian cycles) and test on 64 unseen
graphs. Fig. 2 shows that the test AUROC for y_(t) rises smoothly with S.
There is no sharp transition at S = k, where the AUROC is 0.69–0.72. The state
needed for a given AUROC grows approximately linearly in k. Its median ratio
to k is 2.0, 2.7 and 4.0 at AUROC thresholds 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95; an AUROC of
0.99 is reached for only 7 of the 9 lags. Below the bound, all 22 LTI cells
lie under the optimal AUROC of the Gaussian surrogate with explained variance
S/k (Fig. 3). We checked the surrogate against walk structure with logistic
regression on all pairwise coincidences among the S most recent tokens, which
is what a shift register can compare. For S ≤ k − 3 these predict lag-k
revisits at AUROC 0.49–0.51, i.e. not at all. Within two lags of k they reach
0.53 (S = k − 2), 0.58 (S = k − 1) and 0.67 (S = k), because a closed walk
that leaves and returns along the same path makes stored tokens coincide. The
surrogate is therefore fair for S ≤ k − 3. The input-dependent step changes
the AUROC by  − 0.001±0.002 (mean over cells, |Δ| ≤ 0.03; Wilcoxon p = 0.005,
paired t-test p = 0.14), a negligible difference. Its learned Δ_(t) varies by
only 1% across tokens, which is expected because random features give the gate
nothing to select on.

Controls. Three controls separate the information limit from training effects
(Fig. 3).

-   Shift register. A trained shift register (nilpotent A, learned w,
    k ∈ {4, 8, 12, 16}) has AUROC 0.51–0.62 at S = k and 1.00 at S = k + 1.
    This is the switch predicted by Proposition 1(c). The excess over 0.5 at
    S = k matches the pairwise coincidences above.

-   Frozen poles. We freeze the poles at the delay fit of Fig. 1 and train
    only B and the readout. The AUROC is 0.87 at S = k and 0.97 at S = 2k
    (mean over k), against 0.70 and 0.85 with learned poles. For k ≤ 12 it is
    already 0.92–0.97 at S = k. At k = 16 it drops to 0.64±0.26, where the
    fitted poles are ill-conditioned (Remark 1), and it reaches 0.95 only at
    S = 2k, half the state that learned poles need.

-   Comparison features. Removing them changes the k = 8 AUROC by at most 0.02
    at any S.

For k ≤ 12 the information limit is therefore nearly attainable at S ≈ k, and
most of the overhead of trained SSMs comes from learning the dynamics, not
from the readout. At k = 16 the conditioning of the optimal realisation
accounts for about half of the overhead.

CSL classification. CSL graphs are 4-regular and featureless, so 1-WL is
provably at chance (10%). Each training example is one NB walk (T = 128) on a
CSL graph, and the class is predicted from mean-pooled features. At test time,
each graph is judged from 16 walks (T = 256). As an exact-count reference we
use logistic regression on the exact revisit frequencies at lags 1, …, S. It
is not an upper bound. At S = 2 it is at chance, because NB walks cannot
return at lags 1–2, while the LTI model reaches 24.0%. The reference reaches
98.3%. For all 10 classes, its onset of 80% class accuracy is the first tested
S ≥ W^(*)(r) (Table I). The trained models reach 60.4±7.9% (LTI) and 55.2±8.5%
(input-dependent step) at S = 64. The largest tested S gives the best accuracy
for both, and the curves are still rising. Neither remedy we tried closes the
gap:

-   A per-lag revisit loss gives 56.2±13.7% at S = 64.

-   Evaluating at the training length (T = 128) instead of T = 256 lowers
    accuracy by 2.5 points.

The input-dependent step is  − 1.7±1.1 points from LTI (paired over S and
seeds). The dynamics are not the bottleneck either. A trained shift register
has exact access to all lags  ≤ S − 1, with the same readout, pooling and
labels. It reaches only 47.2% at S = 9, where the reference with lags  ≤ 8
reaches 96.0%, and 53.6±2.0% at S = 32. On CSL the gap therefore lies in
learning per-lag revisit statistics through the readout and mean pooling from
graph-level labels. Class r = 2 (W^(*) = 3) is learned at S = 2 only by
pooling: with one walk its accuracy is 53.7%, and with 16 walks it is 86.3%.

Conclusion

The pure delay has k unit Hankel singular values. For an order-S LTI state
this gives three limits: for Gaussian inputs, exact detection needs S ≥ k + 1
and a singular state matrix; the explained variance of the lagged sample is at
most S/k; and every readout has divergence at most $1/2\ln\frac{k}{k-S}$.
Optimal fits and a shift register attain these limits. Trained diagonal SSMs
respect them but need several times the minimal state. For k ≤ 12, frozen
optimal poles remove most of this overhead. On random-feature inputs, where
the gate stays inactive, an input-dependent step does not change it. On CSL
graphs, even exact lag access through a shift register leaves trained models
far below exact counts, so there the bottleneck is learning revisit statistics
from graph-level labels. Lower bounds for input-dependent dynamics and
better-conditioned delay parameterisations are natural next steps.
